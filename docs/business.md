# TraceMint — the business

How this makes money, who it is for, how they find it, and what has to be true for it to work.
Written to be read by someone deciding whether to fund it, join it, or use it.

Status as of 23 September 2026: working product on GenLayer Studio Next (chain 61997), contract
`0xA7225195035c80Fc6E9128112947B9bF87c7A5Dd`, live at <https://tracemint.vercel.app>. Testnet. No
paying users yet. Everything below marked **assumption** has not been measured.

---

## 1. The problem, stated plainly

An illustrator's work ends up on a shop page, a blog, an advert. The illustrator finds out by accident,
months later. Their options today:

| Option | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Do nothing | Most people do this. It is the default. |
| Send an email | Ignored more often than not. No leverage. |
| DMCA takedown | The image comes down. Nobody gets paid. The use is erased, not licensed. |
| Hire an enforcement agency | They recover money and keep 30–50% of it.¹ Slow, adversarial, minimum case sizes. |
| Hire a lawyer | Costs more than the licence is worth below roughly four figures. |

¹ Published commission rates for Pixsy, Copytrack and similar recovery services sit in that band.
**Verify current rates before quoting this figure publicly.**

The gap is the small claim. A £40 licence is not worth a lawyer, not worth an agency's minimum, and not
worth an argument. So it goes uncollected, and the site owner — who would often have paid £40 up front
if asked — ends up as an infringer by accident.

There are two customers in that sentence, not one.

## 2. What TraceMint sells

**To the creator: collection without confrontation.** Register the work, publish your own terms and
price, list the pages you want watched. An agent checks those pages. GenLayer validators judge each
candidate copy against your terms. An agreed unlicensed copy becomes a notice with a fee already
calculated. You never write an email.

**To the site owner: a way out that is cheaper than a fight.** The notice link shows the work, the page,
the validators' reasoning and the exact number. Pay it and you hold an on-chain licence for that use.
Dispute it with a URL that shows permission and the dispute is judged the same way the claim was. No
letterhead, no lawyer, no threat.

The product is not enforcement. The product is a **settlement rail** that both sides can check.

## 3. Why this needs GenLayer, and not a database

This is the part that decides whether the business exists.

The whole thing turns on one question: *why would a site owner voluntarily pay a notice generated by a
robot?*

If the verdict comes from TraceMint's own server, the answer is: they wouldn't. It is one company's
opinion about their page, produced by a model they cannot inspect, with a bill attached. That is
indistinguishable from a scam, and it is exactly why automated copyright notices have a bad name.

GenLayer changes what the verdict *is*. A leader validator reads the live page, compares it with the
registered work, and proposes a verdict against terms the creator published in advance. Other
validators independently repeat the work and vote on whether that verdict is reasonable. Only an agreed
verdict is written to the contract, and every step is a transaction anyone can open in the explorer.

So the site owner is not being asked to trust us. They are being shown a judgement that independent
parties reached and recorded, about a page they can look at themselves, priced by a formula that was
public before their page was scanned.

No other chain does this. Ethereum can hold the money and the terms, but it cannot read a web page or
weigh "is this the same picture". A centralised AI service can do the reading but cannot make the
judgement checkable. GenLayer's non-deterministic consensus is the only piece that makes an AI verdict
something a stranger can rely on — and "a stranger can rely on it" is the entire product.

**This is also the honest limit.** A TraceMint notice is not a legal claim. It has no jurisdiction and
no court behind it. Payment is voluntary. What it has is a cheap, checkable, low-conflict path to
settling something that would otherwise be dropped. That is enough to build on, and overclaiming it
would be the fastest way to lose the site owners' side of the market.

## 4. Revenue model

### 4.1 What exists today

The contract takes **3% of a licence fee it collects**. The creator gets 97%, credited to their address
and withdrawn directly. Registering, watching and scanning are free beyond network fees.

That 3% is written into `contracts/license_hunter.py` (`PROTOCOL_FEE_BPS = 300`) and shown on
`/pricing`. It is deliberately low: the point of the demo is that the rail costs almost nothing
compared with a 30–50% recovery commission.

### 4.2 Why 3% alone is not a business

Be blunt about the arithmetic. If the average settled licence is $50, 3% is **$1.50**.

Meanwhile the cost driver is scanning, not settling. Each scan run pays several validators to fetch a
page and run a model on it. Most scans find nothing — that is the normal, healthy case, and it still
costs money. So the expense scales with *pages watched* and the revenue scales with *licences settled*,
and those two numbers are not proportional.

A business priced only on settlements would subsidise heavy watchers with light ones and lose money on
anyone whose work is never copied.

### 4.3 The model that follows from that

Price the cost driver, keep the settlement fee low.

| Line | What the customer pays for | Why it prices correctly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Protocol fee — 3% of a settled licence** | The rail and the on-chain record | Near-zero marginal cost. Stays low so settling is obviously cheaper than arguing. Already shipped. |
| **Monitoring tiers** | Pages watched × scan frequency | This is the real cost. Free tier: a handful of pages, scanned weekly. Paid tiers raise page count, works, and frequency. |
| **Portfolio and agency plans** | Bulk works, API access, priority scans, reporting | Rights holders with hundreds of images have the same need at a different scale and can pay per seat. |
| **Front-loaded licences (planned)** | A licence bought *before* publishing | The contract already holds published terms and prices. Selling a licence to a willing buyer is a better business than collecting from an unwilling one, and can carry a higher take rate — 5–10% — because it is a sale, not a recovery. |

The fourth line is where this should end up. Enforcement is the wedge; licensing is the market.

### 4.4 The number that decides everything

**Cost per page per scan on GenLayer mainnet.** Nobody knows it yet, because the product has only run
on a testnet where GEN is free.

Until it is measured, every projection here is arithmetic on a guess. The first job after this
hackathon is to measure it — validator count × model call × web fetch, per page, per run — and then
work out how many pages a $10/month tier can honestly include. If that number comes back too high, the
answer is fewer scans on a schedule, batching many images per page read, and cheaper models for the
first pass with expensive ones only on candidates. If it comes back very high, the monitoring tier
becomes an enterprise product and the free tier becomes a one-off scan.

State this in every investor conversation before anyone else finds it.

## 5. Who to sell to first

Not "creators". That is not a market, it is a word.

**First segment: working illustrators and photographers who already have a portfolio site and already
sell licences.** Roughly: a Behance or ArtStation presence, a personal domain, prints or commissions,
between a few hundred and a few tens of thousands of followers.

Why this segment:

- They already have a price in mind, which is the one input the product needs.
- They already know their work gets reused, and they already have a folder of screenshots.
- Registration requires proving the wallet on a portfolio page — they *have* a portfolio page.
- They talk to each other in public, constantly.

Deliberately not first: large stock agencies (long sales cycles, existing enforcement vendors), NFT
projects (the copy problem is different and the audience is saturated with tooling), and casual social
media posters (no price, no site, no willingness to charge).

## 6. Acquisition

### 6.1 The loop that does the work

Every notice is a product demo delivered to the exact person who needs a licence.

```
creator registers a work
      ↓
agent finds a copy, validators agree
      ↓
notice link reaches the site owner
      ↓
site owner reads a verdict they can check, pays 40 GEN instead of arguing
      ↓
site owner now knows (a) this exists, (b) their other pages are exposed
      ↓
site owner buys licences up front next time — and some of them own work too
      ↓
creator gets paid, tells other creators
```

Two things make this a real loop rather than a diagram. The creator's own self-interest pays for the
distribution. And the site owner arrives already convinced that the problem is real, because they are
holding the evidence.

Nothing has to be spent to reach the buyer side of this market. That is unusual and it is the strongest
thing about the plan.

### 6.2 The way in: a free scan that gives away the answer

The lead magnet writes itself. **Paste your portfolio URL. Get a report of where your images appear.**
No wallet, no signup, no payment.

Most people will read the report and leave. That is fine — the report *is* the marketing, and a
creator who sees four real copies of their work does not need to be persuaded of anything after that.
The call to action at the bottom of the report is one line: register these works and start collecting.

This should be built next, because it converts the product's core value into something shareable. A
screenshot of "we found 6 copies of your work" travels; a landing page does not.

### 6.3 Channels, in priority order

1. **Creator communities where the complaint already happens.** r/photography, r/graphic_design,
   r/Illustration, ArtStation and Behance forums, illustration Discords. The move is not an ad. It is
   showing up in the threads where someone has just found their work on a shop page, and running the
   free scan for them in public. Ten of those, done properly, seeds the first fifty users.
2. **Creator-economy newsletters and YouTube.** People who make a living explaining the business side
   of art to artists. One honest walkthrough video from a trusted voice beats a month of posting.
3. **Site owners who paid a notice.** They are the warmest lead the product has: they have felt the
   problem from the expensive side. Follow up once, with a licence-first offer, never a second notice.
4. **The GenLayer ecosystem.** Grants, the Agent Tank showcase, ecosystem calls, the docs. Small
   audience, but it is the audience that funds and integrates infrastructure. Cheap to serve.
5. **Search.** Slow to start, best long-term margin. Section 7.
6. **Direct outreach to rights holders.** Photo agencies, illustration collectives, university art
   departments. Only worth it once cost per scan is known, because they will ask.

Not worth doing: paid social to creators (they do not buy tools from ads), generic web3 Twitter
(wrong audience entirely), token incentives (they buy mercenary users, and this product needs users
with real work to protect).

## 7. Search, and being the answer

### 7.1 Fix the domain first

The site is on `tracemint.vercel.app`. A subdomain of a shared hosting provider will not rank for
competitive commercial queries, and it reads as temporary to anyone evaluating the business. Buying
`tracemint.com` (or the closest good match), pointing the canonical there and 301-ing everything is the
single highest-value SEO action available and costs about £10.

Everything else in this section is worth less than that one step.

The site is already built to move: canonicals come from `NEXT_PUBLIC_CANONICAL_URL` in
`frontend/lib/seo.ts`, so a custom domain is one environment variable. The old `licensehunter.vercel.app`
alias already points at the same pages, which is why one canonical origin matters now rather than later.

### 7.2 The queries worth owning

Two audiences search very different things.

**Creators, before they know a product exists:**

- "someone used my photo without permission"
- "found my art on a website what can I do"
- "how much should I charge for image licensing"
- "reverse image search my portfolio"
- "image copyright infringement small claim"

**Site owners, after a notice arrives — high intent, almost no competition:**

- "received a copyright notice for an image on my site"
- "is [product] copyright notice legitimate"
- "do I have to pay an image licence demand"

The second group is the one to win. Someone searching that phrase is in the middle of a decision worth
money to us, they are frightened, and the honest answer — here is what this is, here is what it is
not, here is how to check it yourself, here is what happens if you ignore it — is a page nobody else
wants to write. Write it. It also does more for trust than any amount of marketing copy.

### 7.3 Pages to build, in order

| Page | Query it answers | Why it earns the ranking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `/pricing` — **shipped** | "what does it cost", "commission rate" | States the numbers, with the multiplier table read straight from the contract. |
| `/faq` — **shipped** | the seven questions people actually ask | Full-sentence answers with FAQ schema. |
| A licence-fee calculator | "how much should I charge for image licensing" | A free tool that answers the question instead of describing it. Genuinely useful, therefore linkable. |
| "I received a notice" explainer | the whole second cluster above | Nobody else will write this honestly. High intent, near-zero competition. |
| "What to do when someone uses your art" | the first cluster | The long guide that earns links from creator communities. |
| Glossary — licence, notice, dispute, validator, prominence | definition queries | Feeds answer engines the vocabulary the rest of the site uses. |

### 7.4 Answer engines

People increasingly ask a model instead of a search box. Being quotable is a different job from ranking,
and it needs three things.

**Facts in one place, stated once.** `frontend/lib/seo.ts` holds the description, the FAQ set and the
structured data, and `frontend/public/llms.txt` states the same facts in plain text for crawlers. The
pricing table on `/pricing` is generated from the same constants the contract uses, so the site cannot
contradict the chain. One source, no drift.

**Answers a model can lift without editing.** Full sentences with the number in them. "TraceMint takes
3% of a licence fee it collects; the creator receives 97%" survives being quoted. "Industry-leading
low fees" does not.

**Being the primary source.** The contract address, the chain id and the explorer links are on the
site. An answer engine asked "is TraceMint real" should be able to find something it can check rather
than something it has to believe.

Already shipped: `llms.txt`, `robots.txt`, a sitemap, WebSite / SoftwareApplication / FAQPage / Service
structured data, canonical URLs on one origin, per-route titles and descriptions, an OG image.

### 7.5 What not to do

Do not generate a public, indexed page for every notice. It is tempting — thousands of pages, real
data, free traffic — and it is a bad idea. A notice names a third party's domain next to an
accusation that has not been settled, and turning that into a permanent search result is both unfair
and a liability. Notice detail pages are `noindex` for that reason; the index of notices is public,
each detail page is for whoever holds the link.

Do not chase volume with thin programmatic content. The defensible position here is being the only
site that explains this honestly, and that does not come from a template.

## 8. The plan, in phases

**Phase 0 — now. Prove the mechanism.**
Done: works register, the agent scans, validators agree, notices issue, licences settle, earnings
withdraw, disputes resolve. All on chain, all verifiable by a stranger in five minutes at `/judges`.

**Phase 1 — next 3 months. Find out what it costs and whether anyone pays.**

- Measure cost per page per scan on real economics. Everything depends on it.
- Ship the free portfolio scan report, without a wallet.
- Get 50 creators from Phase-1 communities to register real work.
- Measure three rates: copies found per portfolio, false positives, notices settled.
- Buy the domain.
- Write the two explainer pages and the calculator.

The honest kill criterion: if fewer than roughly one in ten notices settle, the voluntary-payment
premise is wrong and the product has to become something else. Better to learn that in month three.

**Phase 2 — 3 to 9 months. Turn enforcement into licensing.**

- Monitoring tiers, priced from the measured scan cost.
- Buy-a-licence-first flow: a site owner finds a work, pays the published price, no notice needed.
- A CMS or image-host integration that checks a picture before it is published, rather than after.
- Reduce false positives with a cheap first pass and validators only on candidates.

**Phase 3 — 9 months onward. Be the rail, not the app.**

- An API other people build on: platforms, print shops, marketplaces, agencies.
- Portfolio plans for rights holders with real catalogues.
- Multi-chain settlement if buyers want to pay in something other than GEN, with verdicts staying on
  GenLayer because that is the part that needs consensus.

Sequencing rule: nothing in Phase 2 starts before the cost number and the settle rate from Phase 1
exist. Building tiers on a guessed unit cost is how this fails while looking busy.

## 9. What could kill this

| Risk | Why it matters | What reduces it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scan cost exceeds what monitoring can charge | The whole model collapses | Measure first. Batch pages, cheap first pass, scheduled rather than continuous scanning. |
| False positives | One wrong notice does more reputational damage than ten right ones earn | Consensus threshold, a free dispute path that already works, human review before a notice goes out at low confidence. |
| Being read as a copyright troll | The comparison is PicRights, and it is unflattering | Licence-first positioning, notices that are cheap and calm, never a second notice to someone who has paid, and no legal threats we cannot make. |
| Site owners simply ignore notices | Payment is voluntary and there is no court behind it | Make paying cheaper and easier than thinking about it. Track the settle rate as the top-line metric. |
| Creators do not care enough to register | The default really is "do nothing" | The free scan report shows them the problem before asking for anything. |
| GenLayer mainnet timing, cost or capacity | Single-network dependency | Stay close to the ecosystem, keep the agent and contract boundaries clean, know what the fallback looks like even if it is worse. |
| Legal exposure from automated accusations | Real, in several jurisdictions | Notices are findings, not claims. Say so on every page. `noindex` on notice details. Take-down on request. Never imply court action. |

## 10. Metrics that matter

Ignore signups. Six numbers tell the whole story:

1. **Cost per page per scan** — the denominator of everything.
2. **Copies found per portfolio scanned** — is the problem as big as creators think?
3. **False positive rate** — the trust budget.
4. **Notice settle rate** — does voluntary payment work? The single most important number.
5. **Days from notice to payment** — how much friction is left in the flow.
6. **Repeat registrations per creator** — did the first payout make them come back?

## 11. Numbers to verify before using any of this publicly

- Current published commission rates for Pixsy, Copytrack, ImageRights and PicRights.
- Size of the image licensing market, from a source with a date on it.
- GenLayer mainnet cost per validator per LLM call and per web fetch.
- Realistic average licence fee for the first segment — ask twenty illustrators what they charge.

Nothing in this document should be presented as measured until it is.
